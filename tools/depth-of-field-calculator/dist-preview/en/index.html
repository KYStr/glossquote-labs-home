Near limit, far limit, depth of field and hyperfocal distance

The circle of confusion is a conventional value — the largest blur spot still seen as a point under standard viewing conditions — not a law of physics. For enlarged viewing or large prints, use a smaller custom circle of confusion.

How it is calculated

Standard thin-lens formulas (Wikipedia, Hyperfocal distance): hyperfocal distance H = f² / (N × c) + f; near limit Dn = s × (H − f) / (H + s − 2f); far limit Df = s × (H − f) / (H − s), and the far limit is infinite when the focus distance s reaches or passes the hyperfocal distance; total depth of field DoF = Df − Dn, also infinite when the far limit is infinite. Outputs are in metres, rounded half-up to 3 decimal places once, at output; the total depth of field is the unrounded far limit minus the unrounded near limit, then rounded.

The listed circle-of-confusion values follow Wikipedia’s “Circle of confusion diameter limit based on d/1500” table: 1-inch 0.011 mm, Four Thirds 0.015 mm, APS-C 0.018 mm, APS-C (Canon) 0.018 mm, APS-C (Nikon) 0.019 mm, APS-H 0.023 mm, full frame 0.029 mm, 645 0.047 mm. The 44 mm × 33 mm digital medium format is not in that table, so enter its circle of confusion as custom (d/1500 is about 0.037 mm, for reference only, not a listed value).

Frequently asked questions

What is the circle of confusion?
The largest blur spot still seen as a point, set by sensor size.
What does an infinite far limit mean?
The focus distance has reached or passed the hyperfocal distance, so everything from the near limit onward is acceptably sharp.
